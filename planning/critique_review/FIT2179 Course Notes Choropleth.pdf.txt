CHOROPLETH MAPS
A choropleth map uses geographic area 
marks with colour channels. It can show a 
single categorical attribute with the hue 
channel, or a single quantitative attribute 
with the brightness channel. 
COLOUR FOR QUANTITATIVE 
CHOROPLETH MAPS 
•Primarily brightness changes: the greater 
the value, the darker the colour. 
•While brightness is the main channel, it is 
common for choropleth maps to also vary 
saturation and hue within limits to in-
crease the number of distinguishable 
colours, and also for aesthetic reasons. 
•Diverging distributions combine two hues 
(for an example, see Granularity of Areas). 
•Varying saturation is possible, but should 
not be the main channel. 
An exception applies for quantitative choro-
pleth maps with a dark background, where 
greater values can be shown with brighter 
values. 
STANDARDISING ATTRIBUTE VALUES 
It is often important to standardise raw 
quantitative values to compensate for the 
differing size of areas in a choropleth map. 
Showing raw values without standardisation 
in choropleth maps is a common error. 
To see why standardisation matters, consid-
er a choropleth map with only two areas. 
Assume both areas are the same size (1 km²) 
and have the same number of residents 
(1,000). The left-hand schema below illus-
trates the resulting map when showing raw 
values. 
Now suppose the left area is split into two 
areas of 0.5 km² each, with 500 people liv-
ing in each. Because the raw values are 
smaller, the new areas are brighter (below 
right). 
Yet there is no reason for the map to show a 
different pattern because no resident has 
moved. If the number of residents in each 
area is divided by its size, the two schematic 
maps below show population density cor-
rectly, both before and after the subdivision.

TERMINOLOGY 
Choropleth combines two Greek words: 
choros means area or region, and pleth or 
plethos means multitude, as in plethora.

This is an example illustrating the need for 
standardising raw values. The two choro-
pleth maps show the urban area of Boston 
in the USA. The purpose of the maps is to 
show the distribution of population. The 
viewer should be able to identify areas with 
dense and sparse populations. 
The map on the left shows raw population 
counts. The number of people counted in 
each area is directly mapped to brightness. 
This map is wrong and misleading because it 
does not compensate for the differing size 
of the areas. This map is not useful because 
it cannot show the pattern of population 
distribution. 
The map on the right shows population 
density: the number of people divided by 
the size of each area. This is the correct 
map, as it clearly shows areas with dense 
and sparse populations. Clusters of dark 
brown colour with high population density 
are easy to identify. 
More generally, standardisation means divid-
ing a raw count by something that makes 
values comparable across regions. Standard-
isation by surface area is only one choice. 
Counts are often divided by population (giv-
ing a rate per capita) or by a total (giving a 
percentage). Which denominator to use de-
pends on what the map should show.

Many quantitative choropleth maps show

PROJECTIONS FOR CHOROPLETH MAPS 
Choropleth maps showing a large geograph-
ic area, such as a large country, a continent, 
or the entire world, should use a projection 
that does not distort area. The Mercator 
projection or any other projection that adds 
considerable areal distortion is a poor choice 
for a choropleth map because the enlarged 
areas visually dominate the map.

GRANULARITY OF AREAS 
Sometimes data is available at different 
geographic granularities, and the map au-
thor gets to choose granularity or level of 
detail. The series of maps on the right show 
population growth. 
 
The ﬁrst map shows European countries. 
The map is unable to show interesting pat-
terns because there are only a small num-
ber of areas, and the areas have vastly dif-
ferent dimensions. The following maps in-
crease the granularity and are able to show 
more interesting patterns. For example, the 
fourth map shows that the eastern part of 
Germany lost population, while the south-
ern part of Germany increased in popula-
tion. The last map shows data at the mu-
nicipality level, where patterns across larger 
areas are difﬁcult to identify, but the 
changes in population of individual cities are 
more clearly visible. 
Hence, the purpose of a map will determine 
what level of granularity it should show.
