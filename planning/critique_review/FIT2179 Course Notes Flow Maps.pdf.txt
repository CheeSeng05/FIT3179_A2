FLOW MAPS
Flow maps show movement. There are three 
main types: origin–destination ﬂow maps, 
maps that show the geometry of paths, and 
maps that show vector ﬁelds. 
Origin–destination ﬂow maps are spatial 
Sankey diagrams of networks; the geometry 
of links is not relevant. 
 The second type does show real geometry, 
as in a map of ﬂight paths. The third type 
visualises vector ﬁelds, for example air 
movement drawn with tightly packed ar-
rows. 
ORIGIN–DESTINATION FLOW MAPS 
Origin–destination ﬂow maps show ﬂows in 
a network as straight or curved lines. They 
do not aim to show the exact geometry be-
tween origin and destination locations; the 
focus is on the quantity or quality of what 
ﬂows through the network. 
Line width can be varied to indicate quanti-
tative attributes. A line does usually not rep-
resent a single item, but the sum of items 
ﬂowing between two nodes. 
The map shows telecommunication trafﬁc, 
measured in minutes, between European 
countries. The exact position and geometry 
of the telecommunication infrastructure is 
irrelevant. Only the amount of communica-
tion matters, so the author is free to arrange 
the ﬂow lines in whatever way optimises 
readability. 
Up to about 50, or perhaps 100, ﬂows can 
be shown on a single origin-destination ﬂow 
map. Beyond that, overlapping ﬂows tend to 
produce cluttered maps that are difﬁcult to 
read.

VECTOR FIELD FLOW MAPS 
The third type shows vector ﬁelds: spatial 
ﬁelds containing direction information and 
possibly further attributes. Fields can be 
two- or three-dimensional.  
The algorithms behind these visualisations 
extract ﬂow directions from the vector ﬁeld, 
tracing a line by following the directions 
stored in the ﬁeld’s cells. The extracted lines 
are then visualised either by placing a 
streamline glyph along the line, or by smear-
ing the pixels of a raster image along its di-
rection. Both techniques can produce aes-
thetic and effective results. The ocean cur-
rent map uses an algorithm that selects 
streamlines so that they are evenly spaced. 
The pixel-smearing method appears in an 
interactive globe at earth.nullschool.net, 
which is enjoyable to explore. There, the 
ﬂow visualisation is combined with other 
scalar ﬁelds, such as air temperature, encod-
ed with colour.
