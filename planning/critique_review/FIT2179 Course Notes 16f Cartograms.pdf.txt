AREA CARTOGRAMS
Area cartograms vary the size of areas so 
that each area is proportional to a quantita-
tive attribute. This requires an optimisation 
algorithm that iteratively adjusts the size of 
the areas, and a variety of such algorithms 
have been proposed. Colour is often used to 
show a second quantitative or categorical 
attribute. Area cartograms are unusual eye-
catchers that grab the attention of readers. 
Area cartograms differ from traditional maps 
in that they do not use a map projection. 
The projection is effectively replaced by an 
algorithm that distorts space in order to ad-
just the size of area marks. There are three 
main types of area cartogram: contiguous, 
non-contiguous, and Dorling cartograms. 
CONTIGUOUS CARTOGRAMS 
Contiguous cartograms distort areas without 
breaking the connections between adjacent 
areas. Every area keeps its neighbours, but 
the resulting shapes can be highly distorted. 
This type has become popular; worldmap-
per.org holds a large collection of examples. 
 
NON-CONTIGUOUS CARTOGRAMS 
Non-contiguous cartograms also scale areas 
in proportion to the attribute value, but the 
shapes themselves are not otherwise dis-
torted. The scaled areas are arranged so that 
the original spatial arrangement is retained 
as far as possible, but they no longer touch 
one another. 
 
DORLING CARTOGRAMS 
Dorling cartograms, named after their inven-
tor, replace each area with a circle whose 
area is proportional to the mapped value. 
The circles are arranged so that the neigh-
bourhood relationships between the original 
areas are retained as far as possible.

READING CARTOGRAMS 
Two difﬁculties affect area cartograms. 
The ﬁrst is that readers need to be familiar 
with the size and shape of the undistorted 
areas. Readers locate places on a map by 
recognising familiar outlines, but contiguous 
cartograms can deform outlines beyond 
recognition in order to encode the attribute. 
Non-contiguous cartograms keep each 
shape intact and so remain the most recog-
nisable of the three. Dorling cartograms dis-
card shape entirely, and depend on the 
reader being familiar with the arrangement 
of the areas. 
The second difﬁculty is size comparison. The 
attribute is encoded in area, and readers 
systematically underestimate how much 
larger one area is than another. This affects 
contiguous cartograms most, because areas 
with irregular outlines are harder to compare 
than the regular circles of a Dorling car-
togram.
