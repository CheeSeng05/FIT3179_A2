TYPOGRAPHY
Typography is the technique and art of ar-
ranging type to display text and other visual 
elements to convey meaning in a legible and 
pleasing way. 
A typeface inﬂuences how easy information 
is to read and how it is perceived, for 
example, how its trustworthiness is judged. 
Selecting a typeface is only one aspect that 
inﬂuences readability and perception of a 
visualisation or text. We also need to 
consider the spacing of lines (leading), the 
visual contrast with the background, 
alignment of text, and many other factors. 
For data visualisation, we need to consider 
how we encode data attributes with type-
related visual channels, such as font size and 
font weight, how we create a visual hierar-
chy to guide the user through a visualisation, 
and how we place labels to unambiguously 
annotate features on maps and diagrams. 
There is something to be said for aesthetics: 
Aesthetic pleasure is an important aspect of 
any visualization. Aesthetics affect the 
usability and effectiveness of a visualisation 
and have the potential to engage viewers. If 
type is ugly or difﬁcult to read, then readers 
will likely not spend much time with a 
visualisation, or ignore it altogether. 
The latin script (also called Roman script) is 
the writing script used for English and many 
other languages. A large number of other 
scripts, such as Arabic, Cyrillic, Chinese, or 
diverse Indic scripts exist. Not all concepts 
and principles discussed in this note transfer 
to these other scripts. 
THE GEOMETRY OF TYPE 
The baseline is the invisible line on which 
the glyphs rest. It is used to measure vertical 
distances. 
The x-height is the distance between the 
baseline and the imaginary line at the top of 
the lowercase x. The x-height deﬁnes how 
high lowercase letters without ascenders (x, 
m, n, o, etc.) are compared to uppercase 
letters. Typefaces with tall x-heights tend to 
have better legibility at small font sizes. 
Leading is the space between adjacent lines 
of type. It can be deﬁned as the distance 
between two baselines. 
Typography measures dimensions in points. 
One point is 0.353 mm (/ of an inch).

TERMINOLOGY 
The terms font and typeface are closely related and sometimes used 
interchangeably, for example, Frutiger bold italic is both a typeface 
and a font. But font and typeface are not synonyms: A typeface is 
what you see, and a font is what you use. For example, when dis-
cussing the form of letters, we refer to a typeface, but a web page 
loads and uses a font ﬁle to display text.

READABILITY OF TEXT
READABILITY AND LEGIBILITY 
A goal of typography is to make text and 
graphic information easy to read. But what 
makes text easy to read?  
Legibility is a precondition for readability. If 
the glyphs are not legible or decodable, the 
reader is unable to read and understand the 
information in the text. Letters and words 
have good legibility when decoding is effort-
less and fast. For letters and words to be 
legible, the reader must be able to easily 
perceive them. 
WHAT MAKES A LEGIBLE TYPEFACE? 
A legible typeface uses generic letter forms 
that are familiar to the reader. The glyphs of 
an ornamental or experimental typeface are 
likely difﬁcult to recognise and decode, and 
therefore have poor legibility. 
Glyphs also need to be easy to distinguish 
from each other. For example, the  lower 
case a and o, or c, e and o letters are difﬁ-
cult to distinguish with some typefaces. 
WHAT INFLUENCES READABILITY? 
Readability is inﬂuenced by legibility, but also 
the complexity and style of the text, the 
reading skills and expertise of the reader, as 
well as the context of the reader. That is, the 
quality of the illumination, electronic display, 
viewing distance, and other factors that can 
support or distract from reading. 
LEGIBLE TYPEFACES 
Early serif typefaces, such as Garamond and 
Bembo, were designed for books. They tend 
to be less fatiguing for long texts than sans 
serif typefaces. Short texts, such as letters, 
explanations for visualisations, road signs, 
labels on maps, or legends and annotations 
on charts are commonly set with sans serif 
typefaces that have been optimised for fast 
reading of short text. A good example is the 
Frutiger type family used for this text.  
ROW LENGTH 
If a line of text is too long, the reader has 
difﬁculty ﬁnding the start of the next line. 
For good readability, a line should not con-
tain more than approximately 60 characters. 
FONT SIZE AND LEADING 
For print, a font size of 9 or 10 points is rec-
ommendable. On digital displays and for 
typefaces with a small x-height, the font size 
should be 11 or 12 points for good legibility.

READABILITY AND LAYOUT
This column is too wide and leading (the space between adjacent lines) is much too small. This results in a block of text with extreme-
ly poor readability. The amount of required leading depends on the vertical extent of the glyphs of a typeface and the number of 
words on a line. With too little leading (as with this text), the lower end of descenders of characters like y, p, or g can intersect with 
the upper end of ascenders of characters like f, l, or k. For legible text, this must be avoided. Also, if a line is too long (as with this 
text), the reader’s eye has to travel a long distance from the end of the current line to the beginning of the next line, and it is likely 
that the reader continues on the wrong line. Additional leading increases readability by reducing the chance of this happening. So 
how many words should there be on a line to make it easy for the reader’s eye to jump from one line to the next line? Adjusting the 
width of a text block such that there are between seven and ten words on a line generally results in good readability. In general, a 
longer line with many words requires more leading than a short line with few words.

The size and weight of text can be varied to 
visualise ordered data attributes. Italic, upper 
case and colour hue can be varied to show 
different categorical data attributes. 
SIZE 
Varying the size of words uses the size 
channel, so varying type size is appropriate 
for visualising ordered data attributes. 
WEIGHT 
Varying the weight of type can also visualise 
an ordered attribute. More ink indicates a 
greater attribute value. For example, a bold 
label on a map indicates a larger or more 
important town than a thin label. 
ITALIC 
The combination of Italic and regular type 
can indicate a categorical attribute with two 
possible values. For example, on maps, italic 
styles are often used to indicate mountain 
peaks or passes, and regular styles are used 
to indicate populated places.  
UPPERCASE AND MIXED CASE 
Uppercase can replace mixed case to indi-
cate the variation of a categorical attribute 
with two possible values. For example, maps 
can label capitals entirely with uppercase 
letters and other locations with mixed case. 
COLOUR HUE 
Varying colour hue can visualise a categori-
cal data attribute. Varying brightness and 
saturation of text is not effective, because 
such variations would result in weak colour 
contrast with the background colour and 
reduce readability of the text. 
COMBINATIONS 
It is possible to combine variations of size, 
weight, italic style, upper case and hue for 
visualising data attributes. This is useful for 
visual emphasis. For example, the size and 
weight can both increase or decrease for an 
ordered attribute, or italic/regular and hue 
can both vary for categorical attributes.

LICENSING FONTS
Fonts, like other digital goods, can be duplicated 
and shared. Fonts are licensed similar to 
computer software, so being aware of the terms 
and conditions is important. The end-user license 
agreement deﬁnes how a font can be used, for 
example, for print, web, apps, or e-books and the 
maximum number of users.  
Google Fonts provides an easy-to-use collection 
of many high-quality free fonts. When used on a 
webpage it comes at the price of sharing the 
client’s IP address with Google.
