DATASET TYPES
DATASETS 
A dataset is a collection of information. 
There are four essential dataset types: ta-
bles, networks, spatial ﬁelds and spatial 
geometry. These four dataset types are 
made up of data attributes. 
A dataset is not inherently visible. To visu-
alise a dataset, we select and apply a visuali-
sation method that transforms the invisible 
dataset into a visual representation. For each 
dataset type a range of visualisation meth-
ods exist. For instance, for table datasets, 
we can choose from various methods, such 
as a bar chart, a line chart, or a pie chart. 
However, not all methods are equally efﬁ-
cient or effective for a given table dataset. 
Understanding the characteristics of both 
attributes and dataset types is essential for 
selecting a visualisation method that ensures 
the data can be read effectively. 
TABLES 
A basic table consists of cells with values 
arranged in rows and columns. Each row 
represents a data item, and each column 
represents an attribute.  
When a table is stored in a database, each 
item is associated with a key that allows for 
unambiguous identiﬁcation and retrieval of 
that item. A multidimensional table has mul-
tiple keys to index each cell. For example, 
we could build a three-dimensional table 
storing house prices for different years. The 
three-dimensional table would have two 
keys: one key to index the house and one 
key to index the year.  
Columns (attributes) and rows (items) of a 
table can be reordered, deleted, added, 
modiﬁed, etc. These operations are useful, 
for example, to sort items in a table by an 
attribute value, or ﬁlter items of interest.

TERMINOLOGY 
Synonyms for attribute are variable and 
data dimension (or just dimension). The 
term dimension has many other mean-
ings; to avoid ambiguity we prefer at-
tribute and variable.
TERMINOLOGY 
The textbook “Visualization Analysis and 
Design” by T. Munzner uses visualisation 
idiom instead of visualisation method. 
TERMINOLOGY 
A data item (a row in a table) is also 
called a record of a table.
TERMINOLOGY 
In visualisation, data is commonly treated 
as a singular noun, whereas in the natural 
sciences, data is often a plural noun.

NETWORKS AND TREES 
A network encodes the relationships be-
tween items. An item in a network is re-
ferred to as a node or vertex. A relationship 
is called a link or edge. 
Examples of network datasets include social 
networks where people are nodes and 
friendships are links; food webs, where ani-
mals are nodes and predator-prey relation-
ships are links; networks of hyperlinks, 
where webpages are nodes and hyperlinks 
are links; and air-trafﬁc networks, where 
airports are nodes and ﬂights are links. 
The nodes and links of a network often have 
attributes. For instance, in an air trafﬁc net-
work, the nodes may have attributes such as 
the name of the airport and the number of 
runways, while the links may have attributes 
such as the number of daily ﬂights and the 
number of yearly passengers. 
A tree is a network without cycles. Each 
node has only one parent node, and nodes 
can have multiple child nodes. Examples of 
trees include ﬁle system hierarchies, which 
organise folders and ﬁles; biological taxon-
omy trees, which classify life forms from 
kingdoms down to species; and organisa-
tional charts, which represent the hierarchy 
of a company or other institutions. 
FIELDS 
A ﬁeld contains values from a continuous 
spatial domain. The values of a ﬁeld are 
measurements or the results of a simulation. 
Fields typically store values in cells, often 
square in shape and arranged at a regular 
interval in a uniform grid. However, more 
complex forms of ﬁelds also exist. For in-
stance, the size of cells can vary to capture 
more detailed information in speciﬁc areas, 
or square cells can be replaced with irregu-
larly shaped triangles that form a mesh sur-
face. 
Fields can have two or three dimensions. A 
two-dimensional ﬁeld with square cells is 
similar to a raster image composed of pixels, 
but instead of storing colour values, each 
cell stores attribute values. A three-dimen-
sional ﬁeld with a regular grid can be imag-
ined as a Rubik’s cube with many cells. In 
analogy to two-dimensional pixels, the cells 
of a three-dimensional grid are called voxels. 
Voxel models are commonly used in engi-
neering and earth sciences. 
The domain of a ﬁeld is continuous, mean-
ing there are conceptually an inﬁnite number 
of locations where values could be measured

Fields can be categorised into three types 
based on the number and semantic of their 
attributes: 
•A scalar ﬁeld contains a single attribute. 
•A vector ﬁeld contains both directions 
and magnitudes (typically representing 
directed speed or force values). 
•A tensor ﬁeld contains multiple attribut-
es that logically belong together. 
Below are examples of typical ﬁeld types: 
•A CT scan of the interior of a human 
body is a three-dimensional scalar ﬁeld, 
containing measurements of tissue den-
sity that indicate how easily X-rays can 
pass through each voxel. 
•A ﬁeld containing elevation values rela-
tive to sea level, sampled using an air-
borne laser scanner, is a two-dimen-
sional scalar ﬁeld. 
•A ﬁeld with wind velocities computed by 
a weather forecast model is a three-di-
mensional vector ﬁeld. 
•A ﬁeld encoding landcover information 
stores the type of landcover at the loca-
tion of the cells in a two-dimensional 
grid. For example, 0 could represent 
water, 1 could represent forest, 2 could 
represent buildings, etc. 
TABLE VS FIELD 
Let’s explore the differences between tables 
and ﬁelds. A major distinction is that tables 
are non-spatial, whereas ﬁelds are spatial: 
tables are not positioned within a spatial 
coordinate system and they do not contain 
information about the size of their cells. In 
contrast, ﬁelds are deﬁned within a spatial 
coordinate system, and their cells have spe-
ciﬁc dimensions. For instance, the three-di-
mensional cells of a medical CT scan may 
measure 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm, while the two-
dimensional cells of a surface temperature 
ﬁeld for Australia could measure 500 × 
500 m. 
A second major difference between tables 
and ﬁelds lies in the operations each dataset 
type supports. 
A table consists of columns (attributes) and 
rows (items). Each column contains a differ-
ent attribute, such as name, salary or age. 
Columns can be reordered, deleted or 
added. For example, the salary and age col-
umns could be swapped, and the name col-
umn could be deleted to anonymise the 
dataset. Rows (items) can also be reordered, 
deleted or added. For instance, items could 
be sorted by decreasing salary values. 
In short, with tables, operations such as re-
ordering, deleting or adding columns and 
rows result in a modiﬁed dataset that gener-
ally remains useful.  
This is not the case with ﬁelds, which con-
tain samples of a continuous domain, for

whereas doing so with a ﬁeld would disrupt 
the spatial continuity of its values and pre-
vent spatial analysis operations. 
GEOMETRY 
The items in a geometry dataset are points, 
lines, areas or volumes that are positioned in 
a spatial coordinate system. Point geometry 
is dimensionless, representing only a loca-
tion. Line geometry is one-dimensional, al-
lowing movement along the line in one di-
mension. Area geometry is two-dimensional, 
permitting movement along two indepen-
dent coordinate axes within the area. Vol-
ume geometry is three-dimensional, allow-
ing movement along three independent axes 
within the volume. 
Lines, areas and volumes are constructed 
from points. For example, a line is made up 
of a string of points, an area is formed from 
a closed line, and a volume is created from 
points forming a three-dimensional shape. 
Items in a geometry datasets often have 
attributes. For example, a geometry dataset 
representing the states and territories of 
Australia consists of eight items, each with 
area geometry and attributes such as the 
state’s name and population. 
For practical purposes, a geometry dataset 
often contains only one type of geometry. 
For example, the 500 most populous cities in 
the world (only points) or all the lakes in 
Victoria (only areas). However, a geometry 
dataset can also contain a mix of different 
geometry types.

FIELD VS GEOMETRY 
Field datasets and geometry datasets are 
both spatial, meaning they are positioned 
within a spatial coordinate system. Let’s ex-
plore the difference between them and how 
they can be transformed into each other. 
A ﬁeld contains values from a continuous 
spatial domain. The values may change from 
one cell to the next, and the level of detail 
of the information that the ﬁeld stores de-
pends on its cell size. Consider a satellite 
image with colours in the spectrum visible to 
the human eye. If we signiﬁcantly enlarge 
this ﬁeld dataset, we can see the individual 
pixels (or cells) of the ﬁeld. When we zoom 
out, the pixels become indistinguishable and 
appear as continuous colour gradients. 
A dataset with area geometry, however, is 
different because its shapes create abrupt 
boundaries. Consider an area geometry rep-
resenting the eight states and territories of 
Australia. If each shape is shown with a dif-
ferent colour, zooming in or out always re-
veals the same eight distinct colours, with 
borders that remain sharp and clearly de-
ﬁned. This is because the areas are built 
from strings of point positions. 
It is possible to convert a ﬁeld dataset into a 
geometry dataset. For instance, we could 
create area geometries for groups of cells 
that have identical or similar values. 
Conversely, transforming a geometry dataset 
to a ﬁeld dataset is also possible. For exam-
ple, consider measuring soil contamination 
with mercury within the area of an industrial 
complex. For each measurement, we mea-
sure a contamination value and also deter-
mine the position with a GPS receiver, result-
ing in a point geometry dataset with a mer-
cury concentration attribute. By applying a 
spatial interpolation method, we could esti-
mate the mercury concentration throughout 
the entire area of the industrial complex. The 
result would be a ﬁeld dataset, where cells 
store interpolated mercury concentrations 
that vary continuously. This transformation 
from point geometry to a ﬁeld is useful be-
cause it allows us to visualise the spatially 
continuous nature of mercury contamination 
with methods speciﬁc to ﬁeld datasets, such 
as contour lines or colour gradients.

ATTRIBUTE TYPES
There are two main types of data attributes: 
categorical and ordered. Ordered attributes 
can be further divided into ordinal and 
quantitative attributes. 
CATEGORICAL ATTRIBUTES 
Categorical attributes, also known as nomi-
nal or sometimes qualitative attributes, do 
not have an inherent order. They differenti-
ate between distinct types of items, each 
with a unique name (the term nominal 
comes from the Latin nomen, meaning 
“name”). 
Examples of categorical attributes include: 
•Hair colour (e.g., blonde, brown, black) 
•Ethnicity (e.g., Asian, Hispanic) 
•Countries (e.g., Mali, Sudan, Chad) 
•Car type (e.g., hatchback, sedan, SUV) 
•Postal code (e.g., 2000 for Sydney, 3000 
for Melbourne) 
•Phone number (e.g., 03 9905 4000 for 
Monash University, 03 9035 5511 for 
the University of Melbourne) 
Note that categorical attributes can be nu-
meric, such as postal codes or phone num-
bers. However, performing arithmetic opera-
tions on these numbers does not make 
sense. 
Some categorical attributes are part of a 
hierarchy. For example, the house cat (Felis 
catus) belongs to the Felidae family, which 
belongs to the Carnivora order. Similarly, 
Melbourne is a city in the state of Victoria, 
which is part of Australia.

ORDERED ATTRIBUTES 
Ordered attributes are sequential, diverging 
or cyclic.  
Sequential attributes vary between a mini-
mum and a maximum value. Examples in-
clude age and depth below sea level. 
Diverging attributes have a midpoint (or zero 
value), where values greater than the mid-
point are positive, and values less than the 
midpoint are negative. Examples include 
temperature and altitude. 
Cyclic attributes repeat over a set period. 
Examples include time of day, months of the 
year, seasons, and compass directions. 
Ordered attributes are divided into two 
types: ordinal and quantitative. 
ORDINAL ATTRIBUTES 
Ordinal attributes have an implied order, but 
numerical calculations, such as subtracting 
or computing a mean, are not possible. 
Examples of ordinal attributes include: 
•Clothing sizes (S, M, L, XL) 
•Rankings in a competition (1st, 2nd, 3rd) 
•Grades (HD, D, C, P , N) 
•Days of the week (Mon, Tue, etc.) 
•Highest education (high school, under-
graduate, graduate, PhD) 
•Customer satisfaction levels (dissatisﬁed, 
neutral, satisﬁed) 
QUANTITATIVE ATTRIBUTES 
Quantitative attributes are real numbers and 
integers, allowing arithmetic operations, 
such as calculating the mean value. 
Examples of quantitative attributes include: 
•House prices in AUD 
•Temperature in degrees Celsius 
•Geographic positions in latitude and 
longitude 
Quantitative attributes can be converted into 
ordinal attributes. For example, quantitative 
marks over 80% can be mapped to the HD 
ordinal attribute, marks between 70% and 
80% to D, and so on. However, the inverse 
conversion from ordinal attributes to quanti-
tative attributes is not possible.
