SPHERICAL COORDINATES
The rotation axis of the Earth deﬁnes the 
location of the North Pole and the South 
Pole. Half-circles on the sphere running from 
the North Pole to the South Pole are called 
meridians. There are an inﬁnite number of 
meridians. The globe on this page shows 
meridians with a spacing of 30°. The Prime 
Meridian passes through Greenwich, in Lon-
don and is the origin for longitudinal angles. 
Longitude is measured relative to the Prime 
Meridian and is positive eastwards and neg-
ative westwards. Its range is between −180° 
and +180°.  
The equator is the circle around the sphere 
halfway between the two poles that divides 
the sphere into two hemispheres. The equa-
tor is the origin for latitudinal angles. Lati-
tude is measured starting at the equator and 
is positive towards the North Pole and nega-
tive towards the South Pole. Its range is be-
tween −90° and +90°. 
A parallel is a circle of constant latitude, 
which is parallel to the equator, hence the 
name. As with meridians, there exist an inﬁ-
nite number of parallels. The globe on this 
page has parallels drawn for every 30°. 
Earth is a three-dimensional object, but 
when we assume that Earth is a perfect 
sphere, we need only a two-dimensional 
coordinate system (latitude and longitude) 
to deﬁne any location on its surface.

SIZE OF EARTH 
The size of Earth is simple to remember. 
The metre unit was originally deﬁned by 
stating that the distance between the 
equator and one of the poles is 
10,000 km. The full circumference of the 
globe is therefore approximately 
40,000 km. The radius is 6,371 km.
MAP PROJECTION DEFINITION 
A map projection is a transformation from 
the curved two-dimensional surface of a 
globe to a plane.  
A map projection is normally deﬁned by 
two equations that convert spherical lon-
gitude/latitude coordinates to Cartesian 
XY coordinates.

MAP PROJECTION DISTORTION
Transforming the curved globe surface to a 
plane is impossible without distortion. Un-
derstanding distortion is important when 
selecting a projection for a map. 
There exist two fundamental types of distor-
tion introduced by map projections: areal 
distortion and angular distortion. 
The Mercator projection is an example that 
distorts area (below). Areas are increasingly 
exaggerated towards the poles, which is 
why Antarctica appears enormous For this 
reason, the Mercator projection is not a 
good choice for a world map. The Mercator 
projection, however, does not distort angles, 
which makes it a useful projection when 
angles are measured on a map, for example 
for navigation at sea.
 
Equal Earth is an example of an equal-area 
map projection (below). Equal-area projec-
tions do not distort the relative size of areas. 
For example, Greenland is 14 times smaller 
in area than Africa. On a map using an 
equal-area projection, this ratio is preserved: 
On the map, Greenland is also 14 times 
smaller than Africa. However, this is not the 
case on a map using a projection that does 
not preserve relative areas. For example, on 
a map using the Mercator projection, Green-
land is greatly inﬂated in size because it is 
close to a pole, resulting in Greenland and 
Africa being shown at a similar size. 
A disadvantage of equal-area projections is 
that they distort the shape of landforms. In 
general, shapes are more distorted when 
they are closer to the border of the map. For 
example, on the Equal Earth map, New Zea-
land is rotated and Alaska is distorted.

PROJECTIONS FOR WORLD MAPS
Many projections for world maps exist. Such 
projections either are equal-area projections 
that do not distort area, or compromise pro-
jections that accept a small amount of area 
distortion to show landforms with less de-
formation. 
 
The Robinson projection (above) is a com-
promise projection that distorts both areas 
and angles. It slightly enlarges area towards 
the poles to more clearly show their shapes. 
The Robinson projection is widely used, and 
many people consider it visually pleasing. 
 
The Equal Earth projection (above) is an 
equal-area projection. The Equal Earth map 
projection is very similar to the Robinson 
projection, but areas near the poles are not 
enlarged. Compare, for example, the size of 
Antarctica on both maps. 
 
The Mollweide projection also does not dis-
tort areas. The outer shape is an ellipse, 
which hints at the spherical shape of Earth. 
However, the shape of landforms is consid-
erably deformed near the poles. 
 
The equirectangular projection transforms 
meridians and parallels to straight lines of 
constant spacing. It distorts areas and an-
gles, and is often used to distribute geodata.

PROJECTIONS FOR REGIONAL MAPS
When selecting a projection for a map that 
shows a section of Earth, such as a conti-
nent, a country, or a smaller area, there are 
three classical types of map projections to 
choose from. They are grouped by their de-
velopable surface. A developable surface is a 
geometric shape that can be unwrapped to 
a ﬂat plane. There are three developable 
surfaces: cylinders, cones, and planes.  
Conceptually, a map projection for a region-
al area transforms coordinates from the 
sphere to these developable surfaces, which 
are then ﬂattened (or developed) to create 
the ﬁnal map. In practice, only a few projec-
tion equations use developable surfaces, but 
the concept of developable surface is never-
theless useful for selecting a map projection 
that minimises distortion. 
The resulting groups of projections are 
called cylindrical projections, conic projec-
tions, and azimuthal (or planar) projections. 
When selecting a projection for a regional 
map, the ﬁrst step is to identify the aspect 
ratio of the mapped area: (1) landscape, 
(2) portrait, or (3) square.  
The second step is to identify the central 
latitude of the area: (1) close to the equator, 
(2) between the equator and a pole, or (3) at 
a pole. 
With these two pieces of information, the 
developable surface that most tightly ﬁts the 
mapped area on the globe can be selected.  
The developable surfaces below illustrate 
three examples. When the area shown on 
the map has a landscape aspect ratio and it 
is located along the equator, then a cylinder 
ﬁts the area tightly, and a cylindrical map 
projection is chosen (left). When the area 
has a landscape aspect ratio and is located 
between the equator and a pole, then a 
cone ﬁts the area tightly, and a conic map 
projection is chosen (centre). When the area 
has a square aspect ratio, a plane ﬁts the 
area most closely, and an azimuthal (or pla-
nar) map projection is used (right).

PROJECTION TYPES
The many map projections can be grouped 
into ﬁve categories: the three developable 
surfaces, plus a category for projections for 
world maps and one for special cases. 
•Conic projections are useful for showing a 
section of the globe on a map with a land-
scape orientation. They show lines of con-
stant longitude as straight lines and lines 
of constant latitude as arcs of circles. 
•Azimuthal or planar projections are use-
ful for square-format maps, for showing 
the poles with a spider-web-like pattern of 
meridians and parallels of latitude, and for 
showing a hemisphere.  
•Cylindrical projections are useful for maps 
showing an area along the equator. They 
show parallels of latitude and meridians as 
straight lines intersecting at straight an-
gles. Cylindrical projections can also be 
used with a so-called transverse aspect to 
show an area with a portrait format. 
Transverse cylindrical projections have 
curved parallels and meridians. 
•Pseudocylindrical projections show par-
allels of latitude as straight, parallel hori-
zontal lines, while meridians are curved 
(except for the straight central meridian). 
Most pseudocylindrical projections show 
the poles as lines to reduce the distortion 
of shapes in polar areas. The Robinson, 
Equal Earth, and Mollweide map projec-
tions are examples of pseudocylindrical 
projections. Pseudocylindrical projections 
are only useful for world maps. 
•Many other projections exist that do not 
ﬁt neatly into the four categories above.

WEB MERCATOR
Map Projections
Tableau, Google Maps, OpenStreetMap, and 
many other mapping tools use the Web 
Mercator projection, which is a variation of 
the Mercator projection with a square for-
mat. The Mercator projection is appropriate 
for maps that zoom in on a small area be-
cause then the area distortion across the 
map is not large. However, it is a poor 
choice for world maps because it dramatical-
ly inﬂates the size of polar areas. The ﬁgure 
below shows a map with the Mercator pro-
jection (bright blue) with countries scaled to 
their correct relative size overlaid (dark blue). 
 
The Mercator projection is a cylindrical pro-
jection that shows parallels and meridians as 
straight lines. This is very convenient for de-
velopers of web maps because north is al-
ways oriented upwards, which is not the 
case with many other projections. 
The Web Mercator projection also clips the 
map at latitudes of about ±85°, resulting in 
a square map. A square map is convenient 
to subdivide into regular tiles at a series of 
zoom levels, and a tiled image pyramid.  
Zoom level 0 consists of a single square tile 
of 256×256 pixels showing the entire globe 
within ±85° latitude. Zoom level 1 doubles 
the number of tiles in both directions and 
uses four tiles. The level 1 map is twice as 
large and contains more detail. The same 
tiling scheme is used for level 2 and all sub-
sequent levels.
