VISUALISING SCALAR FIELDS ON MAPS
Scalar ﬁelds store quantitative values from a 
continuous spatial domain. The values are 
measurements or the results of a simulation. 
Terrain elevation is a common example, usu-
ally held as a regular grid in which each cell 
contains an elevation value. However, scalar 
ﬁelds can also store temperature, air pres-
sure, rainfall, pollution concentration, or 
many other continuous phenomena. 
Four idioms for visualising scalar ﬁelds are 
covered: spot values, contour lines, colour 
mapping, and shaded relief. The ﬁrst three 
apply to scalar ﬁelds of any kind. For exam-
ple, temperature in a weather forecast map 
is commonly shown with colour, and air 
pressure with contour lines. Shaded relief, by 
contrast, is mainly used for terrain elevation 
and bathymetry (underwater depth values). 
The idioms are often combined in one map.

A spot value is a small point mark with a 
text annotation indicating the ﬁeld's value at 
a single location. Spot heights or depth 
soundings indicate elevation or depth. They 
are placed at easily identiﬁable locations for 
outdoor navigation, such as highest peaks, 
passes, road intersections, or the surface 
elevation and greatest depth of a lake. 
Unlike the other three idioms, spot values 
sample the ﬁeld rather than representing it 
continuously. They provide an exact value at 
a few selected points but offer no informa-
tion about the ﬁeld between them.

CONTOUR LINES
Contour lines are vertically spaced by a con-
stant contour interval. The horizontal spac-
ing of contour lines shows the rate of 
change of a scalar ﬁeld. On an elevation 
map, widely spaced lines indicate almost ﬂat 
terrain, while lines running close together 
indicate steep slopes. 
Index contour lines are thicker and often 
labeled with the represented value. 
READING CONTOUR LINES 
Regularly spaced contour lines indicate a 
steady slope. In the contour map below, two 
labels indicate the elevation of two thicker

sonable to assume that the lowest elevation 
lies along the bottom of the map and the 
highest at the top.

TERMINOLOGY 
Contour lines are also called isolines or iso-
pleths. Iso is Ancient Greek for equal, so an 
isoline connects locations of equal value. 
Many domain-speciﬁc names exist. Lines of 
equal pressure are isobars, lines of equal tem-
perature isotherms, lines of equal rainfall iso-
hyets, and lines of equal travel time 
isochrones, etc. All are isolines, or simply con-
tour lines.

CHOOSING A CONTOUR INTERVAL 
The contour interval is the difference in val-
ue between adjacent contour lines. It should 
be chosen so that multiples of the interval 
give round numbers. For example, 10, 20, 
25, or 50 are good choices, because their 
multiples sum to 100. The interval must be 
constant across the whole map; a locally 
varying interval would be confusing. 
Index contours are thicker and often labelled 
with their value. They should likewise fall on 
numbers that are easy to calculate with. For 
example, the interval could be 20 metres 
with index contours appearing every 100 
metres, that is, every ﬁfth line. 
The interval must be chosen so that the lines 
are not too dense. Dense contour lines clut-
ter the map without revealing more detail in 
most locations.  
In the pair of maps shown below, the left 
map has far too many contour lines, while 
the interval on the right produces informa-
tive ones. The choice of the interval depends 
on the map scale and on the maximum gra-
dient in the ﬁeld. Mapping the Australian 
Alps calls for a larger interval; mapping the 
comparatively ﬂat Melbourne area calls for a 
smaller one.

COLOUR MAPPING
Contour lines take training to read and are 
often difﬁcult to interpret. Colour mapping 
is one way to make a scalar ﬁeld easier to 
read. Values are mapped to a colour in a 
gradient, with each pixel coloured according 
to the value at that location. 
Rainbow colour gradients vary hue, which is 
a poor choice for showing a quantitative 
scalar ﬁeld, as illustrated below. 
A typical gradient for mapping elevation 
runs from green at low elevations to beige 
and brown tones at high elevations, imitat-
ing the colours found in temperate zones. 
However, green for low elevations does not 
work well for arid zones, where readers of-
ten misread the green colour to indicate lush 
vegetation. Cartographers have therefore 
developed a variety of alternative colour 
schemes to show elevation and depth, and 
are increasingly using colour from aerial im-
agery. These colours show landcover instead 
of elevation. 
The choice of a colour scheme should follow 
the structure of the data: a sequential 
scheme for ﬁelds that run from low to high, 
and a diverging scheme for ﬁelds with a 
meaningful midpoint, such as temperature 
anomalies centred on zero.

Shaded relief shows the brightness of a sur-
face created by a scalar ﬁeld when illuminat-
ed by an imaginary light source, with each 
pixel shaded according to the orientation of 
the surface at that point. A shaded relief is 
often the background of a map. The exam-
ple here was created by hand by specialised 
cartographers and is close to a piece of art. 
Images like it are not used in this form; in-
stead, a shaded relief is lightened and then 
often combined with contour lines and other 
map elements.

The direction of illumination for relief shad-
ing matters. The shaded relief on the left is 
lit from the top left, which gives the best 
results: valleys read as valleys and mountains 
as mountains. The map on the right is lit 
from below, which causes problems for 
many readers, as valleys can appear as 
mountains and mountains as valleys. The 
reason for this inversion effect is that the 
human brain expects light to come from the 
top or top-left. 
Shaded relief is easy to read and creates an 
effective visual impression, but exact values 
cannot be extracted from it as they can from 
contour lines. It conveys only a general 
sense of elevation.

The idioms for showing scalar ﬁelds are of-
ten combined on maps. For example, the 
map of rainfall in Australia uses contour lines 
ﬁlled with different shades of colour. The 
colours are not derived directly from the 
scalar ﬁeld; contours are derived ﬁrst, and 
these are then ﬁlled. 
The map of South America combines shaded 
relief with spot heights and uses color to 
show land cover. 
Such combinations of idioms for scalar ﬁelds 
have the goal to compensate for the respec-
tive disadvantages of each. See the table on 
the next page for a comparison of the dif-
ferent techniques.
