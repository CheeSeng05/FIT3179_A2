COLOUR SPACES
We have seen three colour channels: 
•Luminance varies between dark black 
and bright white. Luminance is also 
called value or brightness. 
•Saturation is how far away a colour is 
from grey. A desaturated colour is grey 
and a fully saturated colour is a pure 
colour. 
•Hue is the position in the spectrum of a 
rainbow. 
For storing, displaying, and computing we 
need colour spaces. Colour spaces are not 
necessarily based on concepts like hue, satu-
ration and brightness. 
RGB 
The most common colour space is the RGB 
colour space. It has three axes and can be 
represented in a cube. It has a red, a green, 
and a blue axis. The values along each axis 
vary between a minimum of 0 and a maxi-
mum value, which is identical for all three 
axis. If we store each value in an 8-bit value, 
then red green and blue vary between 0 and 
255. 
 
0 means absence of light and 255 means a 
maximum of light. A pure red colour has an 
R value of 255 and a G and a B value of 0. 
If all values equal 0, the result is black. Black 
is placed at the corner of the cube above 
that we cannot see. If all values are 255, the 
result is white. This is the corner pointing at 
us. 
The RGB colour space is used by computer 
monitors, web browsers, scanners, and digi-
tal cameras. However, it is not a convenient 
colour space to pick colours in a user inter-
face, for example, when designing a data 
visualisation. 
HSV, HSL, HSB  
The HSV, HSL and HSB colour spaces are 
made for picking colours in a user interface. 
They transform the RGB colour space by 
mathematical transformation to three intu-
itive colour channels. HSV, HSL and HSB can 
be presented as cylinders or cones, and they 
differ by how they transform from and to

PERCEPTUALLY LINEAR OR UNI-
FORM COLOUR SPACES 
The RGB colour space and the derived HSV, 
HSL or HSB colour spaces are not perceptu-
ally linear or uniform colour spaces. 
What does that mean? Assume that you 
move by a distance of 50 units in the RGB 
colour space. Depending on where you start 
inside the RGB cube, the colour will change 
massively, or it will only change a little bit. 
This is a problem when we need to compute 
with colour values, for example, when inter-
polating between two colours. When we 
compute the numerical mean value of two 
colours, we also expect this colour to be 
visually between the two original colours. 
Colour management also requires uniform 
colour spaces. Colour management trans-
forms colour values between devices with 
different colour properties, for example, 
from a video camera to a monitor, or from a 
scanner to a printer. 
CIELAB OR L*a*b* 
The L*a*b* colour space is a perceptually 
linear colour space. If we compute the com-
ponent-wise mean value of two colours in 
this colour space, the resulting mean value is 
also visually between the two original 
colours. There are three axes: 
•L*: luminance axis 
•a: green–red axis 
•b: yellow–blue axis 
The a axis varies between green and red. 
The b axis varies between yellow and blue. 
Note that values can be negative. Green is 
negative and yellow is negative. If the a val-
ue and the b value are zero, then we get a 
colourless, or grey colour. 
Colour Spaces
A perceptual linear or uniform colour space is a 
colour model that seeks to make the colour-
making attributes perceptually uniform, i.e. 
identical spatial distance between two colours 
equals identical amount of perceived colour 
difference.

DESIGNING DATA VISUALISATIONS WITH COLOUR
Colour Design
How should we choose colour when design-
ing data visualizations? To answer this ques-
tion, read the excellent blog post What to 
consider when choosing colors for data vi-
sualization by Lisa Charlotte Muth at 
Datawrapper. The following ideas and ﬁg-
ures are copied from her blog post. 
WHEN TO USE COLOUR 
The ﬁrst recommendation is to not overuse 
colour. For example, we should not use 
more than a handful of hues to indicate dif-
ferent categories. If we use too many differ-
ent colour hues, it becomes very difﬁcult to 
remember and match colours with attribute 
values. In fact, in the example below it is 
better to not use colour at all, and replace 
the legend with labels and annotations.

for the same variables. In the ﬁrst visualiza-

COLOUR CONTRAST 
Make sure the contrast between foreground 
and background colour is visually strong 
enough. For example, gray text on a blue 
background is difﬁcult to read. 
INTUITIVE COLOURS 
Use colours that your readers intuitively un-
derstand. Green means yes, or good. Red 
means no, stop or bad. Also prefer colour 
that can be observed in real life, such as, 
green for forest, and blue for water. Avoid 
stereotypes when selecting colour, such as 
for different human races, religion, or gen-
der. For example, avoid pink for girls and 
blue for boys. 
LIGHT IS LOW, DARK IS HIGH 
Use bright colours for low values and dark 
colours for high values. This is intuitive for 
readers, but there are exceptions. One ex-
ception is when using a very dark back-
ground. With a dark background, bright 
colours can be used to indicate high values. 
Another exception applies to elevation on 
topographic maps, where higher values are 
commonly shown with brighter colours.

HUE IS FOR CATEGORIES 
Avoid using saturation and brightness for 
showing different categorical attribute val-
ues. The main channel should be hue. 
The same applies when using a colour gra-
dient for a quantitative attribute. Use light-
ness as the primary channel and optionally 
vary between two hues. See the page on the 
evil rainbow colour palette later in this 
script.
COLORBREWER 
Building good colour gradients is difﬁcult; use 
ColorBrewer instead. ColorBrewer lets you 
select the number of colours, the colour hue, 
and whether you need a sequential, diverging 
or qualitative colour scheme.  
ColorBrewer can also suggest colours that are 
visible to people with colour vision impair-
ment.

tiple visualizations and build a brand. Below

The map below uses one of many different 
rainbow colour palettes that mainly vary hue 
for showing quantitative information. 
This palette is an extremely poor choice, 
because it is difﬁcult to interpret. When con-
verted to greyscale, it is obvious that bright-
ness is not applied consistently. Light colours 
do not consistently indicate low values and 
vice versa. 
Various user studies have been conducted to 
ﬁnd and evaluate colourful and effective 
alternatives to rainbow colour palettes. 
A study by Liu and Heer (2018) found that 
palettes with a single hue work well. Varying 
hue within limits also works well when 
combined with changing luminance. A suc-
cessful example is the Viridis palette. It 
varies hue among blue, green and yellow, 
but the main visual channel is still bright-
ness. The brightest colours in Viridis show 
the highest values, and vice versa.

COLOUR VISION DEFICIENCY
Colour vision deﬁciency or colour blindness 
affects about 8% of all men. Women are 
rarely affected, because the condition is 
linked to the X chromosome.  
A variety of colour vision deﬁciencies exist, 
but most affected people have difﬁculty dis-
tinguishing red and green. The term colour-
blind is misleading, because they perfectly 
see other colours, and most can still distin-
guish shades of red and green to a certain 
degree. 
The ﬁgure below shows how someone with 
colour vision impairment perceives colour. 
On the left are a few colours that someone 
with normal vision can distinguish. On the 
right are the same colours as seen by some-
one with deuteranopia, a strong red-green 
vision impairment. If the two sides look very 
similar or even identical to you, then you 
have the deuteronopia condition.  
Red-green palettes are particularly problem-
atic in data visiualisation. On the next page 
is an example of a map showing no votes in 
red and yes votes in green. On the right side 
is the map as someone with colour blindness 
sees it. The yes and no votes are indistin-
guishable. 
Below is the same map with a different 
colour palette. The red colours are replaced 
with purple colours, which are seen as blue 
colours by those with colour vision impair-
ment.
