DATA STORYTELLING
To close the gap in data interpretation and 
exploration experienced by end users, 
researchers have deﬁned guidance-based 
computer-assisted processes aimed at 
narrowing this gap. Guidance can be im-
plemented in several ways, such as en-
hancing charts with visual cues, allowing 
users to select from different visualisation 
techniques (e.g., bar charts vs pie charts), 
or guiding users through prescriptive data 
exploration workﬂows using data story-
telling.
Several data storytelling principles can 
be used to convey insights through data 
(Knaﬂic, 2016; Ryan, 2025).
1. GOAL ORIENTED
A data story should have a clear and spe-
ciﬁc goal that guides the selection and 
emphasis of the data that should be visu-
ally highlighted. 
2. RELY ON SUITABLE CHART TYPE
A data story should rely on appropriate 
chart types, as different charts are better 
suited to different purposes.
For example, consider a pie chart that 
shows market share across four suppliers: 
A, B, C, and D. Based on this visualisa-
tion, which supplier appears to have the 
largest market share?
Most people will agree that Supplier B, 
rendered in medium blue at the bottom 
right, appears to be the largest.

Data storytelling is a guidance ap-
proach that helps audiences interpret 
data visualisations. It combines infor-
mation design and “compression” tech-
niques to help audiences understand 
what is most important in a visualisa-
tion. Data storytelling communicates 
key messages clearly and effectively 
through the combination of data, visu-
als, and narrative.

3. AVOID CHARTJUNK OR CLUTTER
A data story should ﬁrst be simpliﬁed by 
removing unnecessary visual elements. 
Decluttering is a critical step in reducing 
the complexity of a visual representation. 
This involves removing elements such as 
excessive headers, borders, gridlines, and 
data points that are not central to the sto-
ry. When deciding which elements should 
remain in a visualisation, it is helpful to 
distinguish between signal (the informa-
tion we want to communicate) and noise 
(visual clutter that distracts from the mes-
sage). 
To help identify these elements, designers 
can apply the Gestalt Principles of Visual 
Perception.
TERMINOLOGY
Gestalt principles provide guidelines for 
understanding how people naturally 
organise visual information. They de-
scribe how individuals perceive pat-
terns, structure, and relationships when 
interacting with visual stimuli.

4. GUIDE ATTENTION
A data story should guide the audience’s 
attention. Only the data points that are 
critical for communicating the story 
should be emphasised using visual or 
narrative elements/cues. 
This can be achieved in several ways:
1)Adding enhancements such as ar-
rows, lines, symbols, or enclosures; 
2)Changing colour, contrast, or thick-
ness; 
3)Annotating salient data features and 
adding headline titles that sum-
marise the message of the story.
To guide the audience’s attention effec-
tively, designers can use preattentive 
attributes.
TERMINOLOGY
Preattentive attributes are visual proper-
ties, such as colour, size, shape, and 
spatial position, that the brain process-
es subconsciously in milliseconds. 
These attributes allow viewers to quickly 
identify patterns, highlight important el-
ements, and interpret information with-
out focused effort.

NARRATIVE VISUALISA-
TION
Telling stories with data differs in impor-
tant ways from traditional forms of story-
telling.
Edward Segel and Jeffrey Heer (2010) 
conducted a literature review on the de-
sign space of visualisations for storytelling 
and identiﬁed seven genres of narrative 
visualisation. These genres vary primarily 
in terms of:
(a)the number of frames—distinct visual 
scenes, multiplexed in time and/or 
space—that each contains, and 
(b)the ordering of their visual elements.
For example, the 
magazine style has 
only a single frame, 
while a comic may 
contain multiple 
frames.
The comic style is inspired in visual lan-
guage of comics. 
See the suggested 
readings at the end 
of this unit for addi-
tional information 
about data-comic 
design patterns.
A multi-view visualisation (“partitioned 
poster”) may sug-
gest only a loose 
order to its images, 
while a comic strip 
tends to follow a 
strict linear path.
Similarly, ﬂow charts imply a speciﬁc order 
and contain clear 
sequences of nar-
rative events.
